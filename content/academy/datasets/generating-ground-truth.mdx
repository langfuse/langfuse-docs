# Generating ground truth for agent datasets

A dataset item is only as useful as its expected output. If the expected output is wrong, every experiment against it rewards the wrong behavior.

In finance, insurance, or healthcare, you often cannot build your first dataset from production traces: an agent that decides on a refund cannot go live just to see what happens. And even live traces come without a correct answer. Someone has to provide it.

<Callout type="info">
New to datasets? Start with [Datasets](/academy/datasets) and [Designing datasets](/academy/datasets/designing-great-datasets).
</Callout>

This page uses one example: **an agent that reads customer emails to a bank and decides on fee refund requests** (approve, deny, or escalate).

## What ground truth means here

Good ground truth follows the current policy, is recorded with who decided it and why, and holds up to a second expert. As Anthropic's guide to agent evals puts it: "A good task is one where two domain experts would independently reach the same pass/fail verdict."<Ref refs={references} id="anthropic-agent-evals" />

One rule runs through this page: **inputs can come from many places, and a domain expert always writes or confirms the expected output.**

## Four approaches

### 1. Existing data

**Historical cases** are the cheapest start: run the agent offline on past refund emails and have an expert compare its output with the recorded decision. Treat each past decision as a draft until an expert confirms it. Even heavily reviewed public test sets contain label errors, at least 3.3% on average across ten well-known benchmarks.<Ref refs={references} id="northcutt-label-errors" /> Tag each case with its policy version, mask PII, and strip leaked answers such as a note saying "refund approved". In one popular coding benchmark, about a third of successful runs had the solution handed to them in the issue text or comments.<Ref refs={references} id="swe-bench-plus" />

**Public benchmarks** such as FinanceBench help you compare models early. Use them as a baseline only: they know nothing about your policy and may already be in a model's training data.<Ref refs={references} id="yang-contamination" />

### 2. Shadow runs

The human team keeps deciding, the agent proposes a decision in parallel, and nobody acts on the proposal. Then you compare. The hard part is the disagreements, because sometimes the human was wrong. A risk software vendor writes: "Some of the most useful findings in these exercises are cases where the agent was right and the original disposition was not."<Ref refs={references} id="oscilar-shadow" /> A review of silent trials in clinical AI found that "Far fewer studies reported verification of outputs against an in situ clinical ground truth".<Ref refs={references} id="tikhomirov-silent-trials" />

Send every disagreement to an expert, sample agreements too, and keep the human blind to the proposal. Only adjudicated cases become dataset items.

### 3. Synthetic inputs

For rare cases, such as an email from a relative of a deceased customer, write or generate the input and have an expert correct the agent's output. **Only the input is synthetic.** Our [financial services post](/blog/2026-07-15-llm-certification-financial-services) states the rule: "The reviewer supplies the correct answer; the pipeline never copies the suspect output as ground truth." Generated cases can also be "out-of-distribution, either too exaggerated or too subtle",<Ref refs={references} id="yan-product-evals" /> so measure real accuracy on real inputs.

### 4. Human in the loop [#human-in-the-loop]

After go-live, the agent proposes and a named person decides. Each decision is a label, so this is a valid but weak ground truth approach: the reviewer always sees the proposal first. A survey of human oversight policies warns they can give "a false sense of security".<Ref refs={references} id="green-human-oversight" /> Track override rates (near zero is a warning sign), insert known-wrong cases, and keep a blind sample.

In every approach, experts record corrections in Langfuse [annotation queues](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/annotation-queues) as a [corrected output](/docs/observability/features/corrections), which becomes the expected output. The edits are data too: "Repeated corrections to the same type of output may point to a recurring weakness in the system."<Ref refs={references} id="grant-thornton-validation" />

## A recommended order

No published source ranks these approaches. This is our recommendation:

1. **Historical cases or shadow runs**, corrected by experts under a calibrated labeling approach
2. **The same real inputs**, labeled by one expert without calibration, for low-risk items
3. **Synthetic inputs** with expert-corrected outputs, for gaps
4. **Human-in-the-loop decisions**, the steady state after go-live

Public benchmarks sit outside this order as a baseline.

## Making labels trustworthy [#making-labels-trustworthy]

**Setup.** Two experts label the first examples independently and agree on the criteria and edge cases. A senior expert settles disagreements. One evaluation vendor advises: "When they disagree, use adjudication rather than majority vote."<Ref refs={references} id="galileo-adjudication" /> [Score Analytics](/docs/evaluation/scores/score-analytics) reports Cohen's kappa between two scores.

**Running.** After that, the process runs more automatically: one expert labels each new item, and a second reviewer checks a small random sample now and then.

Watch for two risks:

- **Anchoring and automation bias.** One study found AI explanations "increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness."<Ref refs={references} id="bansal-explanations" /> Label a share of cases blind.
- **Criteria drift.** Experts refine what "correct" means while they label: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria."<Ref refs={references} id="shankar-validators" /> Re-check early labels once the criteria settle.

For housekeeping, name an owner for the labels, tag every item with its source and policy version, [version the dataset](/docs/evaluation/experiments/datasets#versioning), and keep a holdout set that never tunes prompts or judges.

## Structuring the item in Langfuse

Put the decision your evaluators check in `expectedOutput` and everything else in `metadata`:

```json
{
  "input": {
    "subject": "Refund of overdraft fee on my late father's account",
    "body": "[synthetic email text]"
  },
  "expectedOutput": {
    "decision": "escalate",
    "must_not": ["approve_refund", "share_account_details_with_sender"]
  },
  "metadata": {
    "source": "synthetic_input",
    "labeled_blind": true,
    "second_review": "monthly_sample_2026-10",
    "policy_version": "fee-policy-2026-09"
  }
}
```

## A note on regulation

<Callout type="info">
No regulator prescribes a method for agent ground truth yet. US banking guidance ([OCC Bulletin 2026-13](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-13.html), [SR 26-2](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.pdf)) places agentic AI outside its scope. [FINRA](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/reports/2026-finra-annual-regulatory-oversight-report/gen-ai) and [Article 14 of the EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/) point to human oversight. Your labeling record is the evidence.
</Callout>

# Generating ground truth for agent datasets

A dataset item is only as useful as its expected output. A wrong expected output makes every experiment reward the wrong behavior.

Most teams build a first dataset from production traces. In finance, insurance, or healthcare, you often cannot wait for those traces. An agent that approves a refund or flags a transaction cannot go live just to see what happens, and a live trace still arrives without a correct answer. Someone has to provide it.

<Callout type="info">
New to datasets? Start with

- [Datasets](/academy/datasets), for the building blocks: input, expected output, and metadata
- [Designing datasets](/academy/datasets/designing-great-datasets), for scope, input distribution, and item schema

</Callout>

The running example is **an agent that reads customer emails to a bank and handles requests to refund a fee.** It proposes approve, deny, or escalate, and drafts a reply. A human team handles these emails today.

## What ground truth means here

Ground truth is the expected output you grade the agent against. In a regulated process it has three properties:

- **Two experts would agree on it.** Anthropic's guide to agent evals puts it this way: "A good task is one where two domain experts would independently reach the same pass/fail verdict."<Ref refs={references} id="anthropic-agent-evals" />
- **It follows the current policy.** A refund that was correct last year can be wrong today.
- **It is recorded in a fixed shape**, with who decided it, when, and why.

**Inputs can come from many places. A domain expert always writes or confirms the expected output.**

## Four approaches

Each approach below ends with the same step: a person with domain knowledge writes, corrects, or confirms the expected output. Pick from the data you have, whether a human process already exists, how much expert time you can get, and whether the agent is live. Many teams combine them.

| Approach          | Advantages                                                | Disadvantages                                                  | When it fits                                         | Poor fit                              |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Historical cases  | Real inputs, high volume, no live risk                    | Past decisions can be wrong, outdated, or leaked into the file | Large archive and a stable policy                    | No history, or the policy has changed |
| Public benchmarks | Free, expert-built, comparable across models              | Built for other processes, and may already be in training data | Early model comparison                               | Your own decisions                    |
| Shadow runs       | Real current inputs, no customer impact                   | Needs a human process, and every disagreement needs review     | A manual process the agent can run beside            | No human process, or very low volume  |
| Synthetic inputs  | Covers rare and risky cases before launch, and avoids PII | Inputs can look artificial                                     | Named gaps, especially "should escalate"             | The main accuracy set                 |
| Human in the loop | Ongoing labels after go-live, and a human always decides  | Weakest labels, because the reviewer has seen the proposal     | A live regulated decision, plus the safeguards below | Before launch, or the only reference  |

## Existing data: historical cases

Years of decided fee-refund emails are a starting set. Run the agent on the original inputs offline, and have an expert compare its output with the recorded decision. The [customer support chatbot example](/academy/examples/customer-support-chatbot) builds its first datasets this way.

**A past human decision is a draft until an expert confirms it.** Even heavily reviewed public test sets contain label errors, at least 3.3% on average across ten well-known benchmarks.<Ref refs={references} id="northcutt-label-errors" />

- **Audit a sample.** If the expert often disagrees with the original decision, review every case before it enters the dataset.
- **Tag the policy version**, and drop or relabel cases decided under rules that no longer apply.
- **Strip leaked answers.** A closed file often includes "refund approved", a later reply, or a status field. In one coding benchmark, about a third of successful runs had the solution in the issue text or comments.<Ref refs={references} id="swe-bench-plus" />
- **Mask PII** before the case enters the dataset, and check that the masking worked.

## Existing data: public benchmarks

Expert-built sets such as FinanceBench help you compare models early, as in the [financial services deployment gates post](/blog/2026-07-15-llm-certification-financial-services). Treat them as a baseline next to your own dataset. They carry none of your fee policy or escalation rules, and they may already be in a model's training data.<Ref refs={references} id="yang-contamination" />

## Shadow runs

The agent proposes a decision on each live fee-refund email while the human team still decides, and nobody acts on the proposal. You then compare the two.

**The comparison is the hard part.** A disagreement is often an agent error, and sometimes the human was wrong. A risk software vendor that runs these exercises with financial teams writes: "Some of the most useful findings in these exercises are cases where the agent was right and the original disposition was not."<Ref refs={references} id="oscilar-shadow" /> Clinical AI runs a similar practice called silent trials, and a review of them found that "Far fewer studies reported verification of outputs against an in situ clinical ground truth".<Ref refs={references} id="tikhomirov-silent-trials" /> An agent can match the human and still be wrong.

- **Report agreement with the current process**, per category.
- **Send every disagreement to an expert**, including cases where both were wrong. Only adjudicated cases become dataset items.
- **Sample agreements too.** Both sides can be wrong in the same way.
- **Set the review period and the thresholds** before you look at the results.
- **Keep it a true shadow.** If the human sees the proposal before deciding, see [anchoring and automation bias](#anchoring-and-automation-bias).

## Synthetic inputs with expert-corrected outputs

Some cases will barely appear in the archive: a bereavement email, a second language, a fee you see twice a year. Write or generate the input, run the agent, and have an expert correct the output.

**The input can be synthetic. An expert always writes or confirms the expected output.** A model that writes both is being tested against itself. The [financial services post](/blog/2026-07-15-llm-certification-financial-services) states the same rule: "The reviewer supplies the correct answer; the pipeline never copies the suspect output as ground truth." If a tool also generated an answer, discard or overwrite it.

Use these inputs for named gaps, as [Designing datasets](/academy/datasets/designing-great-datasets) suggests, especially "should escalate" cases. Generated cases can be "out-of-distribution, either too exaggerated or too subtle".<Ref refs={references} id="yan-product-evals" /> Ask the expert to flag inputs that would never happen, and measure real accuracy on real inputs.

## Human in the loop [#human-in-the-loop]

Human in the loop is a valid but weak ground truth approach. The agent proposes, a named person decides, and that decision is a label. Store it with the source `hitl_decision`. Overrides show where the agent is weak. In a regulated process this often stays the setup for as long as the agent runs.

<Callout type="info">
In the [customer support chatbot example](/academy/examples/customer-support-chatbot), the bot eventually answers customers directly. Which ending fits depends on the cost of a wrong answer and on what your regulator and risk team expect.
</Callout>

The reviewer sees the proposal first, so the decision is anchored. A reviewer who approves everything adds no information. A survey of human oversight policies warns they can give "a false sense of security".<Ref refs={references} id="green-human-oversight" /> These labels sit below expert-corrected labels, and they are the steady source after go-live.

- **Track override rates** per reviewer and per category. A rate near zero is a warning.
- **Insert known-wrong cases** and check that reviewers catch them.
- **Keep a blind sample**, where a second expert decides without seeing the proposal.
- **Show the agent's sources** next to its answer.

## Expert correction: the step every approach shares

Experts review the agent output and correct it, and that correction becomes the expected output. Correcting is faster than writing from scratch, and the edits are data too: "Repeated corrections to the same type of output may point to a recurring weakness in the system."<Ref refs={references} id="grant-thornton-validation" /> Human in the loop is the same step, done lightly during daily work.

In Langfuse, review traces in [annotation queues](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/annotation-queues) and save a [corrected output](/docs/observability/features/corrections), one per trace or observation. Use that corrected output as the dataset item's expected output. The main risk is [anchoring](#anchoring-and-automation-bias).

## A recommended order

No published source ranks these approaches. This order is a recommendation.

1. **Historical cases or shadow runs, corrected after a calibrated setup.** Two experts set the criteria on the first examples, then a periodic sample gets a second reviewer. Historical is cheaper. Shadow is current.
2. **The same real inputs, one expert, no calibration step.** Fine for low-risk, clear-cut items.
3. **Synthetic inputs with expert-corrected outputs.** Best for gaps.
4. **Human-in-the-loop decisions.** Valid and weak. The steady state after go-live, and stronger with the safeguards above.

Public benchmarks sit outside this order. Use them as a baseline. A small reviewed set beats a large archive nobody checked. A historical backtest, then a shadow run, then human in the loop, is one path that works. Other orders work too.

## Making labels trustworthy [#making-labels-trustworthy]

A label becomes ground truth after a check.

**Setup.** Two experts define the labeling approach on the first examples: they label independently, agree on criteria and edge cases, and a senior expert adjudicates disagreements. One evaluation vendor advises: "When they disagree, use adjudication rather than majority vote."<Ref refs={references} id="galileo-adjudication" /> Write the agreed rules down. Use Cohen's kappa, because raw agreement misleads when one outcome is common: if 90% of refunds are approved, two reviewers who always say "approve" agree 90% of the time. [Score Analytics](/docs/evaluation/scores/score-analytics) reports Cohen's kappa, F1, and overall agreement between two categorical or boolean scores. Low agreement usually means the policy is ambiguous, which also caps how well any [LLM-as-a-judge](/academy/evaluate/writing-evaluators#validate-the-judge) can be validated.

After setup, one expert labels each new item, often by correcting the agent's output. On a fixed schedule, for example monthly, a second reviewer labels a small random sample and you recompute kappa. If kappa drops, or the policy changes, repeat the setup for that category.

### Anchoring and automation bias [#anchoring-and-automation-bias]

People tend to accept a machine's answer once they have seen it. One study found AI explanations "increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness."<Ref refs={references} id="bansal-explanations" /> A light review can copy agent errors into the dataset. On a **blind sample**, the expert decides before seeing the agent's output. If agreement with the agent jumps once that output is visible, the corrections are anchored.

### Criteria drift [#criteria-drift]

Experts refine what "correct" means while they label: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria."<Ref refs={references} id="shankar-validators" /> Start from the written policy, refine it during setup, and re-check the first batch of labels once the criteria settle. Repeat the check when the policy changes.

### Housekeeping [#housekeeping]

- **Name an owner** for the labels, separate from the people tuning the agent.
- **Write down the sample:** a random share, plus high-risk and rare cases.
- **Tag policy version and source** on every item, and archive items when the policy changes.
- **Version the dataset.** Every add, update, delete, or archive [creates a new dataset version](/docs/evaluation/experiments/datasets#versioning), so an experiment can be rerun on a past version.
- **Keep a holdout set** aside from prompt and judge tuning.

## Structuring the expected output in Langfuse

Put the decision your evaluators check in `expectedOutput`. Put source, reviewers, rationale, and policy version in `metadata`. That follows the [design rule](/academy/datasets/designing-great-datasets) that notes and comments belong in metadata.

One fee-refund item is below. A model wrote the email. After setup, one expert labeled it without seeing the agent output, a second expert agreed in the periodic sample, and the field names are illustrative.

```json
{
  "input": {
    "channel": "email",
    "subject": "Refund of overdraft fee on my late father's account",
    "body": "[synthetic email text, no real customer data]",
    "account_context": { "segment": "retail", "prior_fee_refunds_12m": 0 }
  },
  "expectedOutput": {
    "decision": "no_decision",
    "escalate_to_human": true,
    "escalation_reason": "bereavement_case",
    "required_actions": ["acknowledge_request", "route_to_bereavement_team"],
    "must_not": ["approve_refund", "share_account_details_with_sender"]
  },
  "metadata": {
    "source": "synthetic_input",
    "input_written_by": "llm",
    "expected_output_written_by": "domain_expert",
    "agent_output_seen_before_label": false,
    "labeling_phase": "running",
    "expert_reviewers": 2,
    "second_review": "monthly_sample_2026-10",
    "agreement": "agreed",
    "adjudicated_by": null,
    "rationale": "Sender may not be the account holder. Bereavement cases go to the specialist team under policy section 4.",
    "policy_version": "fee-policy-2026-09",
    "risk_tier": "high",
    "dataset_role": "synthetic gap-fill",
    "date_labeled": "2026-10-12"
  }
}
```

`source` is one of `historical_case`, `shadow_run`, `synthetic_input`, `public_benchmark`, or `hitl_decision`. `labeling_phase` is `setup` on the first examples and `running` afterwards. Most running items have one reviewer. A sampled item adds the second reviewer, and `adjudicated_by` names the senior expert who resolves a disagreement. Kappa describes the set. `agreement` describes one item.

<Callout type="info">
Once the shape is stable, use [schema enforcement](/docs/evaluation/experiments/datasets#schema-enforcement). Items that do not match the JSON Schema are rejected, so every expert adds items in the same structure.
</Callout>

## A note on regulation

<Callout type="info">
No regulator prescribes a method for agent ground truth yet. In US banking, SR 11-7 was replaced in April 2026 by [OCC Bulletin 2026-13](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-13.html) and Federal Reserve [SR 26-2](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.pdf), and the new guidance places generative and agentic AI outside its scope. FINRA's [2026 oversight report](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/reports/2026-finra-annual-regulatory-oversight-report/gen-ai) says firms may consider "human in the loop" oversight for agents. [Article 14 of the EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/) requires human oversight of high-risk systems and names automation bias. Your evaluation record is the evidence: how labels were made, who approved them, and how the agent scored against them.
</Callout>

## Outlook: production

After go-live, watch for new fee types, stale items, and reviewers who rubber-stamp. Overrides, outcome signals such as reversed decisions ([capturing signals](/academy/monitoring/capturing-signals)), and [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis) point to the failures. Label flagged traces in an [annotation queue](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/annotation-queues) with the same care as above. A production trace is a seed for a new item until an expert has labeled it.

## Put it into practice

- **Match the approach to what you have.** An archive, a live human process, a named gap, or a live agent with a person still deciding.
- **Have an expert write every answer**, and audit past decisions before you trust them.
- **Set up labeling once, then sample.** Two experts on the first examples, then one expert plus a periodic second review.
- **Put the decision in `expectedOutput` and the rest in `metadata`.**
- **Measure human-in-the-loop labels** with override rates, known-wrong cases, and a blind sample.

## What comes next

With trustworthy expected outputs, you can run [experiments](/academy/experiments) and build [evaluators](/academy/evaluate/writing-evaluators) against them, and check whether an LLM-as-a-judge agrees with your experts.

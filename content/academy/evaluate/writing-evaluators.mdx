# Writing good evaluators

Once you know what you want to evaluate, the next question is how. What kind of evaluator do I need? How do I translate the metric I want to measure into inputs and outputs? How do I write a good [LLM-as-a-judge](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge) prompt, or good questions for a [decision model](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/decision-models)?

<Callout type="info">
Haven't decided what to evaluate yet? Take a look at 
- [Choosing what to evaluate](/academy/evaluate/choosing-what-to-evaluate), the deep dive on turning failure modes and product goals into a metric set
- [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis), a structured way to find the failure modes in your application worth checking
- the evaluators on the [Langfuse demo project](/docs/demo) and [these example setups](/academy/examples), as they are good templates to start from
</Callout>

We'll first touch upon inputs, outputs, and which evaluator to choose. Then we'll zoom in on model-based evaluators: LLM-as-a-judge evaluators first, since they are the easiest to get wrong, then decision models.

## What kind of evaluator do you need? [#what-kind-of-evaluator]

Evaluators come in different kinds, each with its own pros and cons. For most evaluation tasks, there is one clear right choice.

### Offline vs online [#online-or-offline]

An evaluator can run in two places, and the purpose is different in each:

- **Offline, on experiments.** You run a new version of your application on a dataset, and the evaluator measures your metric on the outputs. The score exists to compare: is the new version better than the current one? This is the [experiments](/academy/experiments) loop.
- **Online, on production traffic.** The evaluator scores live traces as they come in. There is no comparison here, the score exists to watch the trend over time. This is part of [monitoring](/academy/monitoring).

The two are not exclusive. You can measure the same metric in your experiments during development and on production traffic.

### Prefer deterministic evaluators where you can [#make-it-verifiable]

There are three kinds of automated evaluators. Code evaluators are deterministic. The other two are model-based: decision models answer typed questions, and LLM-as-a-judge evaluators write out their reasoning before a verdict. Each has its trade-offs.

|                   | Code evaluator                                                        | Decision model                                                                      | LLM-as-a-judge                                                                          |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cost              | close to free                                                         | cheap                                                                               | expensive                                                                               |
| Speed             | milliseconds                                                          | fast, since it generates no text                                                    | seconds to minutes                                                                      |
| What you get back | the same verdict for the same input, every time                       | a probability for each answer, no reasoning                                         | a verdict with written reasoning, which can vary between runs on the same input         |
| Scope             | limited: structure, state, and comparisons against an expected output | questions whose answers you can list up front: yes or no, categories, rubric levels | broadest: anything you can describe in language, including criteria that need reasoning |

If the thing you want to evaluate is

- visible in your system (a row was written, a ticket was closed, an order was placed), or
- comparable against an expected output you saved ahead of time

a [code evaluator](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/code-evaluators) can often settle the question exactly, and is faster and a lot cheaper to run. Prefer these over model-based evaluators where you can.

An example: an invoice-processing tool that extracts structured fields (vendor, total, due date) from PDFs, evaluated three ways:

This works, but it costs a model call per invoice, and the verdict can differ between runs.

A decision model can do the same reading for less. **The asymmetry of verification<Ref refs={references} id="wei-asymmetry" />**

Many things are hard to produce but cheap to check, once someone has done the preparation. In many cases, if you have an expected output, every future evaluation run can become a cheap deterministic check.

</Callout>

### Decision model or LLM-as-a-judge [#decision-model-or-judge]

When code can't settle it, you need a model to read the output. Which of the two model-based kinds fits depends on what you need back:

- **Pick a [decision model](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/decision-models)** when the possible answers are known up front and the verdict is all you need downstream, for a dashboard, an alert, or a filter. Its low cost makes it the natural fit for high-volume checks, like scoring every production trace instead of a sample.
- **Pick an [LLM-as-a-judge](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge)** when you need to read why a case failed, to debug it or to act on it, or when the criterion takes several reasoning steps to check.

## Translating a metric into inputs and outputs [#inputs-and-outputs]

Once you know what kind of evaluator to use, you'll need to decide what data you're going to serve as input, and what its output will be. While this depends a lot on your use case, a couple of guiding principles apply.

### Granularity → not one "God Evaluator" [#one-evaluator-per-failure-mode]

It might be tempting to create a single judge that rates accuracy, tone of voice, and completeness together on a 1-10 scale. This is sometimes also called a _God Evaluator_<Ref refs={references} id="yan-product-evals" />. The problem with this is that your resulting score does not tell you what to fix.

For model-based evaluators specifically, narrow ones are also easier to build. Getting a model to agree with you on one specific criterion is a much lower bar than agreeing on "quality". Decision models are built around this: each question asks for [one decision](#writing-decision-model-questions).

### Input → actions over words [#outcome-not-claim]

It's fully possible that an agent ends a support conversation with "Your refund of $200 has been processed, you're all set!" while no refund exists. If your evaluators are going off of only a transcript, you might have a lot of false positives. Instead, a check on the refunds table would catch every case. Anthropic's guide to agent evals<Ref refs={references} id="anthropic-agent-evals" /> condenses this into a rule: **grade the outcome in the environment, not the claim in the transcript**.

### Output → prefer binary or categorical [#binary-verdicts]

Prefer a binary or categorical score instead of a scale, for two reasons:

- a pass/fail verdict is easily verifiable. You can count exactly how often the evaluator catches a failure and how often it clears a pass, which is how you will [validate it](#validate-the-judge) later. There is no equivalent test for whether a 7 was the right score
- scale points don't get applied consistently, even for a human it is hard to say what separates a 6 from a 7. LLMs add a quirk of their own on top, favorite numbers: GPT-3.5 has a preference for the number 7, for example<Ref refs={references} id="lee-llm-judge" />

When one event has several mutually exclusive outcomes, use a single categorical evaluator that picks one label per case (`resolved` / `abandoned` / `handed_off`) rather than several overlapping binary ones.

Decision models map onto both directly: a Yes / no question for a binary verdict, a Choice question for a categorical one.

<Callout type="info">
Tying this to _[not one God Evaluator](#one-evaluator-per-failure-mode)_, avoid having multi-select categorical outputs. It can become ambiguous for the evaluator to know how many to select. In this case, it might make sense to split into multiple separate evaluators.
</Callout>

## Writing a good LLM-as-a-judge [#writing-a-good-llm-as-a-judge]

You might come to the conclusion your evaluation task needs a judge. LLM-as-a-judge evaluators can be powerful, but they are also harder to get right.

Let's get into how you can create a reliable one.

### Label real cases before writing the prompt [#label-first]

Evaluation criteria should not be written exclusively from your head. The reason for this is _criteria drift_<Ref refs={references} id="shankar-validators" />: you need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs is what teaches you your criteria.

Take 10 to 20 real cases of the failure mode you want to evaluate and label each one, with a short comment. That should be enough to build a robust judge. If you ran [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis), most of this exists already.

### Write the prompt like onboarding material [#the-judge-prompt]

The bar for a judge prompt: **a new colleague could read it and reach the same verdicts you would**.<Ref refs={references} id="hamel-llm-judge" /> You can divide a judge prompt into five parts:

1. **Context.** What the application does, and the domain knowledge needed to check the criterion.
2. **A precise criterion, including what to ignore.** "Is the response high quality?" is a question two people would answer differently. "The response cites at least one source document. Ignore formatting issues." will get you the same answer every time.
3. **Labeled examples with their reasons (optional).** It can help to add 2 to 4 of your labeled cases, mixing pass and fail.
4. **Reasoning first, verdict last.** Reasoning-first prompting measurably improves judge accuracy.<Ref refs={references} id="yan-llm-evaluators" /> The reasoning is also the first thing you will read whenever you disagree with a verdict.
5. **Give the judge an explicit way out.** Let it answer "unknown" when information is missing instead of guessing.<Ref refs={references} id="anthropic-agent-evals" />

<Callout type="info">
**On labeled examples**

Labeled examples can be useful to clarify what you mean without a very convoluted description. But when the evaluation task is simple enough, labeled examples can be overkill, and will mainly increase the token consumption of your LLM-as-a-judge. Start out without labeled examples in your prompt, and only add them when your judge isn't accurate enough.

</Callout>

### Use the labeled cases to validate the judge [#validate-the-judge]

LLM judges are small AI systems of their own: they inherit the biases of the model they run on, and their prompts start out as untested as any other prompt in your application. So in order to trust your judge, you need to measure it. This is done by a process called [judge calibration](/guides/llm-as-a-judge-calibration-skill), and you can use your labeled examples from earlier to do this.

<Callout type="info">
**Check all possible outputs at least once**

Suppose the failure you are checking for occurs in 10% of cases. A judge that answers _pass_ every time agrees with you 90% of the time, which you could interpret as a good judge, but actually tells you nothing. So check each class separately.<Ref refs={references} id="evals-faq" />

</Callout>

Beware of the _criteria drift_ that was [mentioned before](#label-first). Sometimes, when seeing the reasoning of the judge, you may realize that your initial label of the situation was wrong.

## Writing good decision-model questions [#writing-decision-model-questions]

A decision model needs the same groundwork as a judge: [label real cases first](#label-first), then [validate against them](#validate-the-judge) before you trust the scores. What changes is what you write. Instead of a prompt, you write questions with a fixed set of answers:

- **One decision per question.** "Is the answer correct and polite?" is two questions. All questions in an evaluator are answered in the same call, so splitting them costs little.
- **Define every answer, not only the question.** For a Yes / no question, write down what counts as yes and what counts as no, including the borderline cases you hit while labeling. For a Choice question, describe each option.
- **Give it a way out.** A decision model has no built-in way to say "I don't know". Add an `unclear` or `other` option when the input may not hold enough information to decide.
- **Keep the input focused.** Map only the fields the question needs. Accuracy drops as irrelevant material piles up in the input, which matters for long agent traces.
- **Choose the threshold with your labels.** A Yes / no answer is the probability that the statement is true. Pick the cutoff that best matches your labeled cases, or send a middle band to human review.

<Callout type="info">
**Debugging without reasoning**

When you disagree with a verdict, there is no reasoning to read. Look at the probabilities instead: an answer split between two options usually means their definitions overlap, or the input doesn't hold enough to tell them apart.

</Callout>

## Where to start

1. Do [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis) if you haven't already, and pick one failure mode from it.
2. Check whether a state or a stored expected output can settle it. If yes, write a [code evaluator](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/code-evaluators) and stop here.
3. Otherwise, label 10-20 cases with how you'd expect the evaluator to score them.
4. If the possible answers are known up front and the verdict is all you need, write [decision-model questions](#writing-decision-model-questions). If you need the reasoning, write a judge prompt: context, one precise criterion, a few of your labeled examples, reasoning before verdict.
5. Run the evaluator on the remaining labeled cases, compare, and iterate on it until it is aligned with how you judge the cases.
6. Ship it, and keep reviewing a sample of its verdicts.

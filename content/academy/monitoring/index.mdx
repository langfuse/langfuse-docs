# Monitoring

## How monitoring fits into the loop

[Tracing](/academy/tracing) provides a complete record of what your LLM app is doing — every request, every model call, every tool use. Monitoring is how you make sense of that data. It gives you two things: a continuous view of how your system performs over time, and a way to surface the specific traces worth investigating — errors, user behavior patterns, and cases where something went unexpectedly wrong. Together, they shift you from having data to actually understanding your system well enough to improve it.

## Metrics and signals [#metrics-and-signals]

**[Aggregate metrics tracking](/docs/metrics/overview)** tells you whether things are getting better or worse over time. Cost, latency, evaluation scores — these become trends you can watch and reason about. Did that prompt change last Tuesday improve anything? Is quality drifting as usage grows?

**Signal detection** tells you where to look right now. It surfaces individual traces that are worth investigating — an error, a cluster of retries, a user abandoning mid-conversation. The signal is only useful because it's attached to the specific trace that triggered it. That trace is your starting point for understanding what went wrong.

## Where metrics and signals come from

Both aggregate metrics and signal detection depend on fields attached to observations. A lot of what you need is already there once you instrument properly: latency, token-derived cost, model and route metadata, tool outcomes, and errors typically flow from your client and provider APIs without extra wiring.

Beyond the built-in fields, you add [scores](/docs/evaluation/scores/overview) from two sources: [user signals](#user-signals) your application captures, and quality evaluation, through human annotation or automated evaluators. That data feeds into aggregate charts for tracking trends over time, and into [alerts](/docs/observability/features/alerts) and filtered views so individual traces surface when something crosses a threshold you care about.

<Callout type="info">
There are three types of automated evaluators to attach scores to traces:
- [LLM-as-a-judge](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge) (for quality judgments where you want to read the reasoning behind each verdict)
- [Decision models](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/decision-models) (for high-volume signals or behavioral patterns with a fixed set of answers, like user disagreement)
- [Code evaluators](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/code-evaluators) (for precise checks on observations or experiments, not traces, like whether the response contains a certain word or exceeds a length limit).

More on all three can be found in the [Evaluate](/academy/evaluate) section.

</Callout>

### User signals [#user-signals]

User signals are things users do or say that show how they perceived an output: a thumbs down, a retry, a request for a human, a draft sent without edits. Some are explicit ratings, but most are implicit in behavior, conversation, and outcomes. They are not evaluation: they measure perceived quality at the moment of use, not whether the output was correct.

They matter for monitoring because they are the cheapest way to get a quality judgment on production traffic. Your users judge every output anyway, through what they do next, and they surface failures you didn't think to evaluate for. Negative signals point you to individual traces worth opening and are the natural entry points for [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis), while signal rates on [dashboards](/docs/metrics/features/custom-dashboards) show whether the experience is improving. [Capturing user signals](/academy/monitoring/capturing-signals) covers the kinds of signals, how to read them, and their biases.

## Where to start

Start small and build your monitoring setup from real traces rather than abstract ideas about what might matter.

1. **Start by looking at your data manually.** Read through traces and notice what kinds of things keep appearing. You can't set up useful monitoring before you know what you're looking for.

2. **Use [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis) to surface what's worth tracking.** Error analysis gives you a structured way to find patterns across your traces, the kinds of recurring issues worth turning into automated evaluators you can run continuously.

3. **Think about how your specific application shows failure.** Application-specific implicit signals — a user disagreeing in a support chat, a correction in a process automation flow — are often more actionable than generic scores, and they surface issues without manual labeling.

4. **Treat it as an iterative process.** A working monitoring setup isn't something you configure once and leave. Usage patterns shift, models get updated, new failure modes emerge. Keep refining your setup so you can cut through the noise and stay focused on what actually matters.

## What comes next

When monitoring surfaces something worth investigating, you have a few options: fix it directly if the cause is obvious, capture it in a dataset if it looks like a pattern, or run a structured evaluation if you suspect something systemic. Which path you take depends on how confident you are about the cause.

- [Datasets](/academy/datasets): capturing production traces for evaluation
- [Experiments](/academy/experiments): testing whether a fix actually worked
- [Continuously Improving Agents With Langfuse](/guides/videos/continuously-improving-agents): a workshop on tracing a support agent, setting up online evaluators, and turning findings into datasets

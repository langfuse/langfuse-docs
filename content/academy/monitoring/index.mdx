# Monitoring

## How monitoring fits into the loop

[Tracing](/academy/tracing) provides a complete record of what your LLM app is doing — every request, every model call, every tool use. Monitoring is how you make sense of that data: you decide what to watch, get that data onto your traces, and use it in two ways, to track how your system performs over time and to find the specific traces worth investigating. Together, they shift you from having data to actually understanding your system well enough to improve it.

## What you monitor [#what-you-monitor]

There are three kinds of things to monitor, and each answers a different question about your application.

### Operational metrics [#operational-metrics]

Cost, latency, token usage, and error rates tell you whether your application is healthy and affordable to run. They are the first to show when a model update, a prompt change, or a traffic spike makes your application slower, more expensive, or less reliable, and they are what you set budgets and service levels against. [Optimizing agent cost](/academy/experiments/cost-optimization) shows how to act on them.

### Quality signals [#quality-signals]

Operational metrics don't tell you whether an answer was right. Quality signals measure that against criteria you define for your application, such as whether a response is grounded in the retrieved documents or follows your support policy. They let you keep watching failure modes you already know about, and catch quality drifting as models and usage change. [Choosing what to evaluate](/academy/evaluate/choosing-what-to-evaluate) covers which criteria deserve a standing metric.

### User feedback signals [#user-signals]

User signals are things users do or say that show how they perceived an output: a thumbs down, a retry, a request for a human, a draft sent without edits. They are not quality evaluation: they measure perceived quality at the moment of use, not whether the output was correct. They matter because they are the cheapest way to get a judgment on all of your production traffic, and they surface failures you didn't think to evaluate for. [Capturing user signals](/academy/monitoring/capturing-signals) covers the kinds of signals, how to read them, and their biases.

## How the data gets onto your traces [#how-data-gets-onto-traces]

Everything you monitor ends up either as a standard field on your traces or as a [score](/docs/evaluation/scores/overview) attached to them.

**Standard fields** come with instrumentation: latency, token usage and cost, model, and errors, plus the metadata, tags, users, and sessions you set when [tracing](/academy/tracing). Most operational metrics need nothing more.

**Scores** hold everything that needs a judgment. You attach them in three ways:

- **Manually**, by reviewing traces and [scoring them in the UI](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/scores-via-ui) or working through an [annotation queue](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/annotation-queues). It's slow, but it's how you learn what good looks like, and it gives you labels to check automated evaluators against.
- **With evaluators** that score production data automatically: [LLM-as-a-judge](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge) for quality judgments with written reasoning, [decision models](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/decision-models) for high-volume checks with a fixed set of answers, such as detecting conversation signals, and [code evaluators](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/code-evaluators) for deterministic checks. This is how quality signals scale to all of your traffic. The [Evaluate](/academy/evaluate) section covers them in depth.
- **Via the [API or SDK](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/scores-via-sdk)** when your application captures something, such as a thumbs-down click, an edited draft, or a support ticket that reopens. This is how most user signals are recorded.

## What you do with it [#what-you-do-with-it]

### Track aggregate metrics [#aggregate-metrics]

Aggregates tell you whether things are getting better or worse. Chart cost, latency, quality scores, and signal rates on [dashboards](/docs/metrics/features/custom-dashboards) to answer questions like: Did last Tuesday's prompt change improve anything? Is quality drifting as usage grows? Set [alerts](/docs/observability/features/alerts) on the metrics where a spike needs attention right away, so you don't have to watch the dashboards to catch it.

### Investigate traces with error analysis [#investigate-traces]

Aggregates tell you that something changed, not why. For that, you need the individual traces behind the number. Errors, low quality scores, and negative user signals point you to the traces worth opening, and filtering on them gives you a list to work through. [Error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis) turns that reading into a structured process: it groups failures into categories and measures how often each one happens, so you know what to fix and what to keep monitoring.

## Where to start

Start small and build your monitoring setup from real traces rather than abstract ideas about what might matter.

1. **Start by looking at your data manually.** Read through traces and notice what kinds of things keep appearing. You can't set up useful monitoring before you know what you're looking for.

2. **Use [error analysis](/academy/monitoring/error-analysis) to surface what's worth tracking.** Error analysis gives you a structured way to find patterns across your traces, the kinds of recurring issues worth turning into automated evaluators you can run continuously.

3. **Think about how your specific application shows failure.** Application-specific implicit signals — a user disagreeing in a support chat, a correction in a process automation flow — are often more actionable than generic scores, and they surface issues without manual labeling.

4. **Treat it as an iterative process.** A working monitoring setup isn't something you configure once and leave. Usage patterns shift, models get updated, new failure modes emerge. Keep refining your setup so you can cut through the noise and stay focused on what actually matters.

## What comes next

When monitoring surfaces something worth investigating, you have a few options: fix it directly if the cause is obvious, capture it in a dataset if it looks like a pattern, or run a structured evaluation if you suspect something systemic. Which path you take depends on how confident you are about the cause.

- [Datasets](/academy/datasets): capturing production traces for evaluation
- [Experiments](/academy/experiments): testing whether a fix actually worked
- [Continuously Improving Agents With Langfuse](/guides/videos/continuously-improving-agents): a workshop on tracing a support agent, setting up online evaluators, and turning findings into datasets

Banks, insurers, and healthcare providers handle a lot of routine work: answering the same customer questions, checking documents, summarizing cases. AI agents can take on a large share of it, so people can spend their time on the cases that need judgment. The goal is less routine work, with the same quality and control the business has today.

Getting there takes more upfront work than in other industries. Many teams building AI agents ship early and improve in production. In banking, insurance, and healthcare, that approach rarely gets past the first review. A wrong answer can be a compliance incident, customer data cannot go to just any vendor, and someone has to sign off before the agent talks to a customer.

A common way to start is human in the loop: the agent proposes an answer or action, and a person approves it before anything reaches the customer. Regulators recommend this kind of oversight, and it is a sensible first step. For it to work, the agent's proposals need to be good from the start. If reviewers have to rewrite most drafts, the agent saves no time and people stop trusting it. The bigger payoff comes later, when a reliable share of cases can run without review and the team can measure and trust that autonomy rate.

We will show you how teams in regulated industries get there, from the first prototype to an agent that handles part of the work on its own.

## What makes regulated industries different [#the-challenge]

AI development teams in regulated industries usually face three requirements for their projects.

**Evidence before launch.** Banks already run risk processes that ask for test results, review sign-offs, and documentation before an application reaches production ([deployment gates for LLMs in financial services](/blog/2026-07-15-llm-certification-financial-services)). The review board will want to see how the agent behaves on realistic cases.

**Oversight after launch.** Regulators expect people to stay in control. FINRA's [2026 oversight report](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/reports/2026-finra-annual-regulatory-oversight-report/gen-ai) asks firms to define where humans review agent actions and how they track agent decisions. [Article 14 of the EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/) requires that people can effectively oversee high-risk AI systems.

**Control over data.** Conversations contain account details, transactions, and health information. Teams need to know where that data is stored, what gets redacted, and who can see it. In healthcare, a [business associate agreement](/security/hipaa) has to be in place before any protected health information is processed.

Together, these requirements raise three questions every team has to answer: how do you decide whether an agent is ready for production, where do people need to stay involved, and when can you automate more? The rest of this post walks through how teams answer each one.

## The approach: start the loop offline [#the-approach]

The [AI engineering loop](/academy/ai-engineering-loop) describes how teams improve AI applications: trace, monitor, build datasets, run experiments, and evaluate. And then repeat this process. Teams with a live product usually start by adding tracing to production. Regulated teams need to evaluate and improve their application before going to production. They trace the prototype, build out ground truth with their domain experts, evaluate and improve the application, add monitors, and only then go live.

## Stage 1: Trace the offline application [#trace-offline]

Add [tracing](/docs/observability/get-started) before anyone outside the team uses the agent. The traces you collect serve multiple purposes: datasets are built from traces, evaluators score traces, and reviewers use traces as evidence of what the agent did.

Instrument your application to capture each step your agent is taking. Record the prompt version, model, tool calls, retrieved context, and any human approval on every run. This makes traces from offline tests, shadow runs, and production comparable, and each trace doubles as the decision log a reviewer will ask for.

Decide early where the traces will live. This is where the data control requirement becomes concrete, and regulated teams usually choose between two setups:

- **Self-hosted.** Run Langfuse in your own infrastructure with the [self-hosting guide](/self-hosting), so trace data never leaves your environment.
- **Langfuse Cloud.** Healthcare teams can use the [HIPAA-ready cloud region](/security/hipaa) after signing a business associate agreement. Other regulated companies opt for the cloud version whenever self-hosting is not a must-have.

[DKB](/users/dkb), one of Germany's largest direct banks, made self-hosting a requirement before any production data could be touched. [Trade Republic](/users/trade-republic), a fully licensed bank with over 10 million customers, went the same way. Its Ops Tools team runs self-hosted Langfuse and rolled it out across teams while meeting the bank's compliance requirements.

Also mask sensitive fields before they are stored. The SDKs support [client-side masking](/docs/observability/features/masking), so account numbers or patient identifiers can be redacted at the source.

At the end of this stage, the team can see what happened in every run, explain why the agent responded the way it did, and show that evidence to whoever needs to review it, from a compliance officer to a model risk committee.

## Stage 2: Verify with a starting dataset [#starting-dataset]

Next, build a starting [dataset](/docs/evaluation/experiments/datasets) together with your domain experts. In a bank, that might be a compliance officer or a senior support agent. In a hospital, it is a clinician. The goal is both to show that the agent handles the common cases the way your experts would, and to show that adversarial and edge cases are handled gracefully by your agent.

### Where the cases come from [#where-cases-come-from]

Regulated teams rarely need to invent test cases. There are multiple sources that can be used to collect dataset cases.

- **Historical data.** Run the agent on cases your team has already decided, such as resolved support tickets or reviewed claims, and compare the agent's output with the human decision. Clinical teams call this retrospective back-testing, and [Qualified Health](https://claude.com/customers/qualified-health) uses it as the first step of every deployment.
- **Shadow runs.** Let the agent process live cases next to the current workflow without acting on anything, and compare its output with what the team did. Healthcare calls this silent evaluation, and the [Coalition for Health AI](https://assets.ctfassets.net/7s4afyr9pmov/6e7PrdrsNTQ5FjZ4uyRjTW/c4070131c523d4e1db26105aa51f087d/CHAI_Responsible-AI-Guide.pdf) recommends it before any AI output reaches clinical staff. Shadow runs usually start once the agent does well on historical cases, and they keep running while you set up monitors in stage 3.
- **Work with domain experts.** Domain experts have a good intuition for typical cases, and they also know from experience which cases are hard to handle. Let them write typical cases and setups, including how they expect them to be handled.

Those sources are often combined for an initial dataset of typical cases.

### Check the ground truth [#check-the-ground-truth]

One expert's ground truth is a good start. However, experts disagree more often than teams expect. The more disagreement we see between humans, the more difficult it is for agents to handle the same cases. In qualitative and clinical research, this is called inter-rater reliability: the extent to which different raters assign the same score to the same item. It is usually measured as percent agreement or with Cohen's kappa, and many texts recommend at least 80% agreement between raters ([McHugh, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23092060/)). Langfuse [score analytics](/docs/evaluation/scores/score-analytics) calculates both metrics when you compare two sets of annotations. Ask a second expert to label a sample of the dataset. Where the two disagree, discuss the case, add the missing rule to your guidelines, and update the label.

[Annotation queues](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/annotation-queues) give experts a place to annotate traces without touching code. At DKB, customer service specialists work in Langfuse directly and add typical examples to datasets that engineers then use to adjust prompts.

### Define the no-gos and test the guardrails [#guardrails]

Before launch, agree with compliance on what the agent must never do. For a bank, that could be giving investment advice, quoting a rate that is not on the price list, or sending a customer message without approval.

Build a guardrail for each no-go and test it like any other component. Add dataset cases that should trigger the guardrail and normal cases that should pass through. A guardrail that fires too often gets ignored, so measure both.

### Improve the agent one failure at a time [#improve]

Run the full dataset as an [experiment](/docs/evaluation/experiments/experiments-via-sdk) and record the results as your baseline. The first run rarely meets the bar your experts set. From here, improvement follows a simple cycle:

1. **Find a failure.** Sort the experiment results by score and pick a case that failed, or one your experts flagged.
2. **Understand it through the trace.** Open the trace and find the step where things went wrong. Retrieval may have missed the right document, the prompt may lack a rule, or a tool may have returned outdated data.
3. **Make a change.** Fix the prompt, the retrieval, or the tool, depending on what the trace shows.
4. **Test again.** Rerun the experiment and [compare it with the previous run](/docs/evaluation/experiments/compare-experiments). Check that the failure is fixed and that no other case got worse.

Repeat the cycle until the agent meets the bar, and rerun the dataset every time you change a prompt, model, or tool. Trade Republic takes this a step further when migrating live workflows to new agent architectures. The team iterates on one dataset and keeps a second, held-out dataset as a final exam, and only ships when the new agent passes both.

## Stage 3: Add compliance and user-signal monitors [#monitors]

Set up monitors before launch. The shadow runs from stage 2 are the right place to turn them on first: the agent processes real cases, nothing reaches customers, and you learn what normal looks like. You can tune thresholds and fix noisy evaluators before a single customer is affected.

**Compliance monitors come first.** They check every trace against your no-gos. An [LLM-as-a-judge](/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge) evaluator can flag a response that sounds like investment advice. A code check can flag a missing disclaimer. Set [alerts](/docs/observability/features/alerts) for when the share of flagged traces rises.

Decide upfront who acts on each type of flag. A typical split looks like this:

- **Domain experts** review flagged conversations in annotation queues, rate how serious each one is, and confirm or correct the agent's response.
- **Engineers** take recurring issues, find the cause in the trace, and add the case to the dataset so the fix can be tested.
- **Compliance** gets an alert right away for critical flags, such as a possible data leak or advice the agent must never give.

DKB screens every answer its assistant gives and sends flagged conversations to annotation queues sorted by service area, such as brokerage or credit. Specialists rank each case by severity and handle the critical ones first. For DKB, this screening is part of its license to operate.

Compliance also decides what may be screened at all. DKB is not allowed to auto-review conversations of its own employees or of customers under 18, so it keeps those conversations in a separate Langfuse instance outside automated screening. Check rules like these with your compliance team before you set up evaluators.

**User-signal monitors** matter for chat agents. Track [explicit feedback](/docs/observability/features/user-feedback), and score conversations for signs of trouble, such as a user asking for a person, repeating a question, or correcting the agent. The Academy page on [capturing signals](/academy/monitoring/capturing-signals) lists the most useful ones.

At launch, let the agent escalate a little too often. A reply like "I can't help with that, I'll connect you to a person" costs a few minutes of staff time. A wrong answer in a regulated setting can cost much more. The [AWS Well-Architected Agentic AI Lens](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/agentic-ai-lens/agentops01-bp02.html) suggests three triggers for a handoff: low confidence, high stakes, and an exhausted retry budget.

## Stage 4: Go live and run the loop [#go-live]

Once the dataset passes, the shadow runs match what your experts expect, and the monitors are in place, go live. From here you run the normal loop: trace production, watch the monitors, and turn interesting traces into new dataset items.

### Keep humans on a subset [#humans-on-a-subset]

People stay in the process after launch, and the amount they review shrinks as the evidence grows. A common pattern, described by [Coinbase at AI Engineer NYC](https://ai.engineer/reading/nyc-2026/transcripts/oct14-evan-kormos-policy-workflows.md), has three steps:

1. **Supervised.** Reviewers check every output.
2. **Sampled.** Once measured quality passes a threshold set by policy, reviewers check a sample.
3. **Exceptions.** Reviewers mainly handle flagged cases and keep improving the datasets.

Match the review rate to the risk. Guidance from the [Joint Commission and CHAI](https://assets.ctfassets.net/7s4afyr9pmov/5RX4XUbRg0l1JG0gwXGkTM/bea3c1949d6d8cdd8ec2ae2f82f737ad/JC-CHAI_RUAIH_Guidance.pdf) recommends checking tools that inform clinical decisions more often than tools that help with administrative tasks. The same logic applies in a bank: a credit decision deserves more review than a branch-hours question.

### Automate the slices that work [#automate-slices]

Asking a person to approve everything does not scale, and reviewers who approve hundreds of routine cases a day stop looking closely. The fix is to automate what is clearly routine.

In practice, classify incoming requests by category. Let the agent handle categories that score well in your dataset and in production, such as a password reset, and keep sending the rest to a person. The Academy's [customer support chatbot example](/academy/examples/customer-support-chatbot) walks through this approach, automating only the request categories that cleared the quality gate.

Keep a small random sample of each automated category in an annotation queue. Once humans only see exceptions, the biggest risk is the case the agent failed to flag, and sampling is how you catch quality drift.

### Track the autonomy rate [#autonomy-rate]

This is where the autonomy rate from the beginning becomes measurable. It is the share of cases the agent handles end to end without a person. Track it per category, together with three numbers that show whether quality holds:

- **Quality on sampled reviews.** The share of randomly sampled cases your experts rate as correct.
- **Flag and escalation rate.** How often compliance monitors fire and how often the agent hands off to a person.
- **Review time.** How many hours your team spends reviewing each week.

The agent is saving time when the autonomy rate goes up and review time goes down, while sampled quality and the flag rate stay stable. If quality drops in a category, move that category back to sampled review until the next fix has passed the dataset. You can record each of these as [scores](/docs/evaluation/scores/overview) and chart them in a [custom dashboard](/docs/metrics/features/custom-dashboards), so the same numbers are available to engineering, the business, and the review board.

Finally, treat every change to a prompt, model, or tool as a new release. Rerun the stage 2 dataset, compare it against the baseline, and only then deploy. DKB runs this full loop in Langfuse today.

## Where to start [#where-to-start]

Bringing an agent into production in a regulated industry takes more work upfront. In return, the team ends up with what the review board, the regulator, and the business all ask for: evidence that the agent works before launch, people involved where the risk is highest, and a measured path toward automating more of the routine work.

The same holds outside banking. [Merck](/users/merckgroup) serves more than 80 AI project teams on Langfuse and uses it to keep an audit trail of every AI interaction.

Start with one workflow. Pick one with high volume, clear rules, and low risk per case, such as answering account questions or summarizing support tickets. Trace it, build a starting dataset with your experts, set up monitors, and launch with people reviewing every output. Then expand automation one category at a time, as far as the evidence supports. Much of what you build for the first workflow, from the tracing setup to the evaluators and the review process, carries over to the next one, so each new agent gets to production faster.

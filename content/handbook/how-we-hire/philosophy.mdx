# Hiring philosophy

Who we hire shapes Langfuse more than any plan we write. These are the principles we use when we make hiring decisions. They also tell you what to expect as a candidate. For the steps, see the [hiring process](/handbook/how-we-hire/hiring-process).

## Stay small, keep the bar high

We want to stay as small a team as possible, where everyone is excellent, rather than grow a large team that needs managers to coordinate it. With AI, one strong person can scope, design and ship what used to take a team, so every hire carries a lot of weight. If in doubt, we would rather miss a great candidate than bring on the wrong one.

## Hell yes, on both sides

Someone on our side has to be genuinely excited about you and able to say why. A few soft yeses do not add up to a yes. The same applies to you: if you are not "hell yes, I want to work at Langfuse", you should not join. We work to earn that. Everyone you meet should be able to explain why the role, the team and the work are worth it, and we try to meet you in person.

## Owners, end to end

Engineers own a product area: they decide what to build, ship it, and launch it. Marketing and growth roles are the same shape. The work is writing, and you shape the role you take on.

## What you did, not where you did it

A well-known company or school helps us find people. It does not decide. Someone from a less-known startup who executed well on a difficult problem is exactly who we want to meet.

## We go deep and ask the uneasy questions

We keep asking until we reach the reasoning behind a decision. Expect follow-ups that are not comfortable. That is how both sides find out whether you would enjoy making these calls here.

## AI pilled

Almost everyone here works with agents all day and keeps improving how they do it, see [Productivity & AI](/handbook/how-we-work/productivity-and-ai). We expect the same from you. The question is not whether you use AI but whether you understand what you shipped with it, and the result should be something we would merge.

## Each round stands on its own, and we move fast

Each interviewer judges only what they saw in their own conversation, so one round does not tilt the next. We aim to get from the first call to a decision in about two weeks, because good people have options.

## Interviews should feel like the job

We want both sides to see real work. You work on problems we actually have, with people you would work with, so you can judge us as much as we judge you. Try the product before you talk to us. We wrote the [handbook](/handbook) and [docs](/docs) for this.

The work itself usually looks like this: talk to a customer, write an async RFC on the few hard trade-offs, settle them in one focused discussion, write a lot of code, and launch it together with the go-to-market team. In Europe, we meet for a few days in Berlin every two months, and the whole company comes together at [offsites](/handbook/how-we-work/remote), most recently in Tokyo and Berlin. See our [principles](/handbook/product-engineering/principles) for how we work day to day.

## What we look for [#what-we-look-for]

No candidate checks every box. These are the signals we look for in every conversation, and the reasons we most often say no.

- You love the work. You enjoy building for a technical audience and want to see your work used by thousands of customers.
- Ownership. You drove the outcome yourself rather than coordinating it. You can tell us what you decided, what you would undo, and what happened after launch. If you shipped a bug, you fixed it.
- Depth of thinking. You go several layers below the headline: what happened, the trade-off you chose, what it led to, and what you learned from it.
- Taste and pragmatism. You know what good looks like, when to cut scope and when to go deep, and you choose a better user experience over beautiful technology.
- Customers. You want to talk to the developers using what you build, often, and you let what they tell you change your plan.
- Clear motivation. Every job change has a reason, and you tried to fix the problem before you left. You know why Langfuse and why this role, and you have read the handbook and docs.
- Reading code as well as writing it. Agents write a lot of code now, so reviewing and understanding code matters more than typing it.
- Startup fit. You are comfortable with ambiguity, make many decisions as an individual contributor, and enjoy a small team where everyone is accountable and everyone does support.
- Clear, direct communication. You say what works, what does not, and where you are unsure. Your opinions are based on facts, and you change them when the facts change.

What usually leads to a no:

- Lots of "we" and no "I". We cannot tell what you personally did.
- Motivation that would fit any company.
- Answers that stop at the headline when we ask why.
- Work you built with AI but cannot explain or defend.
- A career of coordinating, consulting or handing work over, without owning a product over time.
- A code review that misses the security, data access or performance problems an owner of that code would catch.

A plus, not a requirement: early startup or founder experience, and open source contributions.
